Why homes feel drafty

Small openings can add up to a whole-home problem.

Air moves through gaps in attics, walls, floors, foundations, and utility penetrations. That uncontrolled airflow can create cold rooms, humidity problems, dust pathways, and higher heating and cooling demand.

Air sealing closes those unintended pathways. Insulation slows heat flow; air sealing controls air movement. In many homes, the two work best together.

Common leakage locations

Air rarely follows the obvious path.

The most important leaks are often concealed between conditioned rooms and attics, crawlspaces, basements, or attached spaces.

Attic penetrations

Recessed fixtures, wiring, plumbing stacks, top plates, dropped soffits, and open chases.

Attic access points

Pull-down stairs, scuttle hatches, kneewall doors, and access panels.

Rim and band joists

Framing transitions along basements and crawlspaces where the structure meets the foundation.

Basement and crawlspace gaps

Utility entries, foundation penetrations, sill plates, and openings around mechanical systems.

Windows and exterior doors

Gaps behind trim, worn weatherstripping, and incomplete connections to the surrounding wall.

Duct and register pathways

Openings around boots and chases; duct leakage itself is evaluated as a related but separate measure.

Tight—and properly ventilated

Comfort should never come at the expense of healthy air.

Making a home tighter changes how air moves through it. That is why EcoPower evaluates combustion appliances, moisture conditions, exhaust systems, and ventilation needs as part of the work.

The goal is controlled airflow: seal unwanted leaks, then provide fresh air where the home needs it.

Common questions

Air sealing, explained.

Is air sealing the same as insulation?

No. Air sealing blocks unintended airflow through holes and gaps. Insulation slows heat transfer through building surfaces. A strong building envelope usually needs both.

Can I just caulk around my windows?

Window and door gaps can matter, but larger leakage pathways are often hidden in attics, basements, crawlspaces, and framing transitions. Testing helps identify where work will have the greatest impact.

Can a home become too tight?

A tighter home may need planned mechanical ventilation. That is why ventilation, combustion safety, and moisture conditions should be evaluated as part of professional air sealing.

Should air sealing happen before insulation?

Yes, in most attic projects. Once insulation covers leakage sites, they become harder to access. BGE’s Home Performance guidance requires air sealing before insulation for qualifying projects.

Are rebates guaranteed?

No. Program eligibility and rebate amounts depend on the home, utility account, modeled savings, selected improvements, project costs, installation requirements, and current BGE rules.
